STR715-Q-021

Question: Study in detail about trends of seismic assessment techniques in Nepal.

Source-scope note

This answer is constructed only from the verified course/source set. The corpus supports a progression in assessment depth and scale, not a strict year-by-year national history. Therefore, “trend” below means the methods and practices represented by the approved Nepal-focused sources, without claiming that every Nepalese agency or practitioner follows the same sequence.

1. Course framing of seismic-assessment trends

The official STR715 outline places qualitative methods and analytical methods under Seismic Assessment. It explicitly includes capacity/demand methods, pushover analysis and inelastic time-history analysis. It then treats fragility curves, empirical/analytical damage assessment, failure probabilities and vulnerability tools in the next unit. This gives the course-level direction: assessment can move from screening and diagnosis to increasingly quantitative performance and risk evaluation.

2. Nepal-specific tiered assessment practice

The Government of Nepal retrofitting guideline provides a direct Nepal-specific assessment framework. Its vulnerability-evaluation annex starts with building-data collection and site verification, then uses qualitative structural assessment to identify seismic deficiencies, and follows with quantitative assessment when the preliminary phase finds deficiencies or uncertain performance. The qualitative stage uses drawings where available, site visits, interviews, typology identification, vulnerability-factor checklists, code-compliance checks and quick checks. The quantitative stage requires more complete structural information, material properties, reinforcement information, hazard definition, performance objectives and detailed analysis.

This establishes an important trend in practice: screen first, investigate and model more deeply only when needed.

3. Shift toward nonlinear performance assessment of Nepalese buildings

verified Nepal-focused RC studies demonstrate the use of nonlinear methods rather than relying only on simple elastic checks. One comparative study of current RC buildings in Nepal used:

  • conventional static pushover with different load patterns;
  • adaptive pushover;
  • nonlinear dynamic time-history analysis;
  • capacity curves, base shear, roof displacement and inter-storey drift as response measures; and
  • drift limits from established assessment guidance to judge safety/performance.

A separate Nepal study of RC frames with masonry infill also used nonlinear modelling, static/adaptive pushover and dynamic analysis, and evaluated strength, stiffness, energy dissipation, inter-storey drift and total displacement. This shows a practice of assessing actual inelastic response and performance indicators, including the influence of masonry infill, rather than treating the frame alone as sufficient.

4. Expansion from single-building assessment to vulnerability and risk

The verified Nepal seismic-risk study extends assessment from an individual building to a wider building stock. It combines:

  • probabilistic seismic hazard;
  • building exposure data;
  • fragility functions and vulnerability functions for different Nepalese building typologies; and
  • consequence/loss modelling in the OpenQuake engine.

The same source notes that Nepal risk work moved beyond a single generic fragility relationship toward damage-state-specific fragility/vulnerability representations for several typologies, including adobe, masonry and RC. This is a clear broadening of assessment from “Will this building perform safely?” to “What damage and loss are likely across many buildings under different shaking levels?”

5. Practical trend summary for Nepal

Within the verified course/source set, the main trend can be summarized as:

6. Key points to write in an exam

  1. Nepal assessment practice represented in the corpus is tiered: qualitative first, quantitative when deficiencies or uncertainty justify it.
  2. More detailed studies use nonlinear static pushover, adaptive pushover and nonlinear dynamic time-history analysis.
  3. Assessment increasingly uses performance measures such as capacity curves, base shear, roof displacement, drift and damage/performance limits.
  4. Nepal-focused studies account for local construction typologies and masonry-infill effects, rather than treating all buildings as identical.
  5. At larger scale, assessment extends to fragility, vulnerability, probabilistic hazard, exposure and loss modelling.
  6. Assessment results are linked to the next decision: acceptable performance, detailed investigation, strengthening/retrofit, or broader risk-reduction planning.

Limitation retained

The source set does not justify a precise national timeline with dates for each method. This answer therefore presents methodological trends evidenced in the verified source set, not an invented chronology.

STR715-Q-022

Question: Study in detail about national guidelines (of 2017) about seismic assessment and retrofit guidelines of masonry, RCC, and Adobe structures.

Mandatory source-year conflict note

The canonical question wording is preserved exactly and says “national guidelines (of 2017)”. However, the verified Government of Nepal source is explicitly titled “Seismic Retrofitting Guidelines of Buildings in Nepal, 2016.” It states that the manual is prepared in three separate volumes for Adobe/low-strength masonry, masonry and RCC. A different verified document dated July 2017 is a New Zealand seismic-assessment guideline and cannot be used as Nepal national authority.

Therefore, this answer does not silently rewrite the question, and it does not invent a Nepal 2017 guideline. It answers the technical content from the verified 2016 Government of Nepal guideline while retaining the 2017-vs-2016 conflict as an explicit provenance issue.

1. Purpose and structure of the verified Nepal guideline

The Government of Nepal guideline is intended to reduce life-safety risk and control earthquake damage in existing buildings. It provides analysis/design methodology for seismic evaluation and retrofitting and separates guidance by construction type:

  • Adobe and low-strength masonry;
  • Masonry;
  • Reinforced concrete (RCC/RC).

The guideline emphasizes that retrofit intervention may occur at element level, but the required performance must be achieved at the global building level. It stresses load path, connectivity, irregularities, brittle failure modes and the overall structural system.

2. Seismic assessment framework before retrofit

The guideline’s vulnerability-evaluation annex provides a two-stage logic.

Qualitative assessment:

  • collect available drawings and building data;
  • verify information by site visit and, where needed, interviews;
  • identify structural system and building typology;
  • inspect damage/deterioration and potential vulnerability factors;
  • check code/detailing compliance and perform quick checks;
  • establish target performance level and identify deficiencies.

Quantitative assessment:

  • follows when the qualitative stage finds deficiencies or uncertainty;
  • requires more reliable structural drawings and member/foundation information;
  • establishes material properties and reinforcement details using investigation and NDT/intrusive tests where necessary;
  • defines seismic hazard and performance objective;
  • carries out detailed analysis to determine whether strengthening is required.

Thus, retrofit is not selected first and justified later; assessment identifies deficiencies, then the retrofit strategy is chosen to correct them.

3. Adobe and low-strength masonry guidance

For adobe/low-strength masonry, the verified guideline focuses on creating integral box action, improving wall stability and strengthening weak connections. Measures described in the source include:

  • seismic belts/bands near lintel and roof/floor levels to hold walls together and improve in-plane/out-of-plane behavior;
  • wall jacketing/confinement, including PP-band systems and external wire-mesh reinforcement;
  • strengthening around door and window openings;
  • stiffening and anchoring floors/roofs/diaphragms to improve transfer of seismic forces to walls;
  • foundation strengthening and tie/belt measures where required; and
  • repair/restoration of damaged wall material and cracks as part of the retrofit process.

The governing idea is to prevent loss of wall integrity, separation at corners/connections, out-of-plane failure and brittle collapse.

4. Masonry guidance

For masonry structures, the source again emphasizes a continuous lateral-load path and adequate wall, diaphragm and foundation behavior. Representative measures include:

  • strengthening masonry walls for in-plane and out-of-plane action using systems such as wire mesh with plaster, PP bands or other suitable wall reinforcement;
  • controlling and strengthening openings, including lintel bands and vertical reinforcement, or infilling unnecessary openings where appropriate;
  • strengthening or stiffening wooden floors/roofs/diaphragms and wall-to-floor anchorage;
  • adding shear walls where needed to increase strength/stiffness while controlling torsional effects; and
  • treating the foundation assessment and strengthening as an integral part of the overall rehabilitation scheme.

The source requires the whole retrofitted wall/building system to remain safe under the governing combination of lateral actions rather than treating local wall repair as sufficient.

5. RCC/RC guidance

For reinforced-concrete buildings, the verified guideline identifies common deficiencies such as inadequate beam-column joint ties/confinement, poor anchorage/lap details, low concrete strength, inadequate lateral stiffness/strength and plan/elevation irregularities. Retrofit strategies are grouped broadly into:

A. Increase seismic capacity

  • strengthen existing columns, beams and joints using RC jacketing, steel-based strengthening and fiber-wrap/FRP-type overlays where appropriate;
  • increase wall/slab capacity or improve slab-wall connections;
  • strengthen/anchor infill walls;
  • add new lateral-force-resisting elements such as RC shear walls, infill walls, wing walls, additional frames, trusses or diagonal bracing.

B. Reduce seismic response

  • increase damping using energy-dissipation devices;
  • reduce mass where feasible;
  • use base isolation or supplemental damping systems where technically and economically justified.

The guideline also describes widely used RC retrofit options such as RC jacketing, added RC shear walls, steel bracing and FRP strengthening and emphasizes reliable connection between new and existing construction.

6. How to present the “2017” issue in an exam answer

A source-grounded answer should state:

The study question says 2017, but the verified Nepal Government guideline available in the approved source set is the 2016 “Seismic Retrofitting Guidelines of Buildings in Nepal,” covering Adobe/low-strength masonry, masonry and RCC. The July-2017 assessment guideline in the corpus is a New Zealand document, so it should not be cited as Nepal national authority.

Then discuss the Nepal assessment framework and the material-specific retrofit measures above.

7. Compact comparison

Limitation retained
